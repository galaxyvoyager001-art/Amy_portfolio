SmartHearing: Scene-Adaptive Hearing Assistance via LALM-Guided Language-Queried Sound Enhancement
Research question. Which sound matters most right now, and how can a hearing aid enhance it in a scene it has never heard before?
Xiangyu Kong & Amy (Wanying) Hu, equal contribution · ICASSP 2027 submission
Abstract  Conventional hearing aids prioritize speech, yet which sound matters most (a siren, an alarm, a scream) depends on the acoustic scene. Speech-centric models suppress non-speech warnings, and scene classifiers rely on predefined labels that do not transfer to unseen environments. We propose a scene-adaptive hearing assistance framework that couples a large audio language model (LALM) for open-vocabulary scene understanding with language-queried sound separation. A lightweight Query-space Adapter bridges the representation shift between LALM-generated descriptions and the separator's training queries, and an adaptive window selects 4–10 s of context per sound category. On 600 mixtures, the Adapter improves SNRi and SI-SDRi by 0.97 dB and 1.14 dB over the base separator; on 145 unseen ESC-50 mixtures, the gains persist (+1.32 dB, +1.19 dB). In a 101-participant listening test, the system was rated most salient in 50.5% of trials versus 28.1% and 21.5% for baselines.

1 · Motivation

Hearing-impaired listeners regularly meet restaurants, shopping malls and multi-speaker meetings, where background noise, mixed sounds and overlapping speech coexist. Conventional hearing aids primarily amplify human voices, so other sounds that carry critical information, such as a car horn on the street, may be attenuated, creating fatal risk.

2 · Method

The perception path uses Qwen3.5-Omni to decompose each 16 kHz window into sound objects ok = (sound, role, danger, confidence, relevance), with six roles from urgent warning to unwanted noise. A frozen CLAP encoder maps each description to a 512-d embedding, and the Query-space Adapter corrects it: e′k = ek + αkAθ(ek). LASS (ResUNet + DPRNN) extracts each source at 32 kHz, and an Audio Processor sets gains by risk and confidence: yt = gbbt + Σ gksk. The two paths run asynchronously, so inference never blocks audio.

3 · Experiments

Adapter V1 is trained on FSD50K; V2 adds ESC-50, UrbanSound8K, NIGENS and TUT Rare Sound Events 2017. Each of seven target classes contributes 500 training and 100 validation mixtures, with ten interferers and SNRs from −10 to 10 dB.

4 · Results

Speech denoisers collapse on non-speech targets (−18.4 dB SI-SDRi for GTCRN) and a fixed query collapses entirely. Prompt engineering alone (10.21 dB) matches base LASS, so the Adapter's gain cannot be replicated by query design. With no ground-truth label, SmartHearing recovers most of the oracle gap. The Adapter raises within-class similarity from 0.785 to 0.860 and cuts the distance to LASS training queries from 0.181 to 0.119. For a siren in wind, residual energy falls 7.05 dB and SI-SDRi rises from 0.16 to 7.21 dB.

An ablation isolates the Adapter: V1 already improves on base LASS, and V2, trained on a larger mixture set, significantly outperforms V1 in most metrics (ΔPESQ +0.61 [0.25, 0.96]). Between-class similarity also rises, from 0.337 to 0.485, which reflects shared convergence toward the region LASS expects rather than a loss of discrimination.

5 · Conclusion

A proper Adapter between perception and execution improves separation of the sounds that matter. Next: cross-checking LALM outputs and evaluation with hearing-impaired listeners.

For a hearing-aid user this means a siren, an alarm or a baby's cry is no longer treated as noise to be removed, even in a scene the system has never been trained on.

6 · Latency and windows

Inference is dominated by the LALM API (median 1.24 s) against only 0.067 s of local separation. A window sweep over ten lengths, six safety sounds and ten interference types (6,000 units) showed that no single window is best: a bark needs about 4 s, a siren or a crying baby 10 s.
