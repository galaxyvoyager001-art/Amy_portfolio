1 · Geometric optics

With the conjugate method the focal length of a convex lens came out at 100.0 mm against a nominal 100 mm. The method only needs the screen distance D and the lens shift d, f = (D² − d²)/4D, which cancels the uncertain position of the lens's optical centre. Three screen distances (550, 600 and 650 mm) gave consistent results.

A calibrated eyepiece micrometer gave 8.40 µm per division and a fibre diameter of 71 µm; the scales had to be recalibrated for every objective. At minimum deviation, a glass prism gave n ≈ 1.517 and showed dispersion: violet bends most, red least. Finding minimum deviation means watching the spectral line reverse direction, so I read each position several times.

2 · Interference

In Newton's rings the air film between a lens and a flat plate produces dark rings with Dm² = 4mRλ. Using two widely separated orders removes the uncertain contact point, and gave R ≈ 0.85 m. I always turned the micrometer drum in one direction to avoid backlash.

An air wedge gave a sheet thickness of about 74 µm by measuring 20 fringe spacings at once; pressing the glass made the fringes visibly move. Young's double slit gave a He-Ne wavelength of about 633 nm from ten fringe spacings, and the Michelson interferometer showed rings appearing from and collapsing into the centre as one mirror moved.

3 · Diffraction

Single-slit Fraunhofer diffraction gave a slit width of 0.20 mm: the central maximum is twice as wide as the others, and the whole pattern widens as the slit narrows. A transmission grating, read on both sides of zero order to cancel offset error, gave the mercury green line at about 546 nm.

4 · Polarisation

Rotating an analyser in 15° steps reproduced Malus's law, I = I0cos²θ, almost exactly; the small intensity left at 90° comes from the finite extinction ratio of real polarisers and stray light.

6 · Refraction & holography

An Abbe refractometer gave 1.3330 for distilled water and 1.3474 for a sugar solution, recording temperature because index changes with it. In static laser holography, the developed plate looked like noise, but lit by the He-Ne beam it rebuilt a 3-D image with real parallax: proof that the plate stored phase as well as amplitude.

7 · What the errors taught me

Every report ended with an error analysis, and the same lessons kept returning: judge the sharpest image or the darkest fringe several times; measure many fringes at once rather than one; read both sides of a symmetric pattern to cancel offsets; turn screws in one direction only to avoid backlash; and record temperature, because it changes refractive index. Those habits are what made the results close to the reference values.

8 · Into the research lab

For holography I recorded with the 632.8 nm He-Ne beam at about 5 mW, an object distance of about 25 cm, a reference angle of about 35°, an object-to-reference intensity ratio of about 1 : 1.5 and a 5 s exposure; even a fraction of a wavelength of vibration during exposure would have erased the fringes.

Amy Hu · nonlinear optics research intern · 12 written lab reports
Summary  I worked through twelve classic optics experiments, from the focal length of a thin lens to static laser holography, and wrote each up as a full lab report with aims, principle, procedure, data and error analysis. The measurements agree closely with reference values: a He-Ne wavelength of about 633 nm against 632.8 nm, the mercury green line at about 546 nm against 546.1 nm, and a normalised intensity of 0.502 at 45° against the cos²θ prediction of 0.500. In the research lab I then operated graduate-level Raman and laser systems, tested more than twenty polarisation states of the beam, and spoke with a Nobel laureate about where experimental physics is heading.
