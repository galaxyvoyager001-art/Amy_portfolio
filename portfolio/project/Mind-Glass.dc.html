Flowed Metal: Micro-particle Impacts on Metallic Glasses under High Temperature
Research question. When a metallic glass is hit at hundreds of metres per second, does heat make it harder, like a metal, or softer, like a glass?
Amy (Wanying) Hu, first author · supervisor Prof. Minqiang Jiang · spacecraft solid mechanics research intern
Abstract  Bulk metallic glasses (BMGs) represent a unique class of materials that combine the disordered atomic structure of glass with the properties of metals. Their response to dynamic loading, such as high-velocity impact, is not only crucial for potential structural applications but also fundamental to understanding non-crystalline physics. Crystalline metals show a counterintuitive phonon-drag strengthening effect under high strain rates at high temperatures, which contrasts with glasses' conventional thermal softening behaviour. Therefore, whether BMGs exhibit a similar phenomenon remains an open question. In this work, the impact response of a Pd43Ni10Cu27P20 BMG at 25 °C, 100 °C and 200 °C was investigated via laser-induced particle impact tests and finite element method simulations. In-situ observation revealed a significant enhancement in energy dissipation of BMGs at 200 °C. Post-impact characterization identified a transition in deformation mechanism: solid-like craters with minimal pile-up at 25 °C gave way to fluid-like, irregular pile-up morphologies at 200 °C, suggesting adiabatic heating-induced softening. A calculated ~10.4% reduction in dynamic yield strength at high temperature provided quantitative confirmation of this thermal softening. Furthermore, simulations incorporating a physical constitutive model successfully replicated the experimental results and uncovered the underlying mechanism: high temperature promotes the activation of shear transformations, leading to greater plastic flow and prolonged impact interaction time. This study conclusively demonstrates that temperature is a critical, governing factor for the dynamic performance of BMGs, challenging simplistic material selection and providing vital insights for their application in extreme environments. Key words: micro-particle impact, metallic glass, temperature, plastic flow, energy dissipation.
1 · Background

Glass is a solid at room temperature but behaves like a super-viscous liquid when hot, because its atoms are arranged with "short-range order and long-range disorder". Metals are crystalline, with long-range periodic order that gives them strength and ductility. Metallic glass combines the two: molten metal is cooled so fast that its atoms have no time to crystallise, freezing the disordered liquid structure into a solid.

Under impact, ordinary glass shatters while metal deforms plastically. Recent work showed that at strain rates above 104 s−1 pure metals (Cu, Au, Ti) become stronger as temperature rises, through phonon drag. Would a metallic glass harden like a metal, or soften like a glass?

2 · Sample

Pd43Ni10Cu27P20 was chosen for its low critical cooling rate and high amorphous stability. Samples (10 × 5 × 3 mm³) were wire-cut with a diamond saw, ground, polished and mounted on a heated glass plate. XRD showed only one broad hump near 40° and no sharp peaks, confirming the glassy state; DSC gave Tg = 570 K and crystallisation at 632 K, so 25–200 °C keeps the sample amorphous.

3 · LIPIT platform

A 7 ns, 532 nm pulsed laser is focused through a convex lens onto a launch pad: 4 mm K9 glass, a 100 nm gold ablation layer and an 80 µm polymer elastic layer carrying 30 µm SiO2 particles. The expanding plasma flings a particle at 1–1000 m/s; a camera at up to 5,000,000 fps records it. A REX-C900 PID controller holds the target from 25 to 400 °C within ±1 K. Craters were then imaged by SEM and their depth profiled by laser confocal microscopy.

4 · Energy dissipation

From impact speed vi and rebound speed vr, COR = vr/vi. At 200 °C the COR is consistently lower: the hot glass dissipates more energy, the cold glass resists penetration better. Between 80 and 250 m/s both follow COR ∝ vi−0.5 (finite plasticity: 8.6 vi−0.5 at 25 °C, 7.7 vi−0.5 at 200 °C); above 250 m/s they fall more steeply, 140 vi−1 against 372 vi−1.2.

5 · Dynamic yield strength

In the finite-plastic regime, Wu's relation COR = α[(vi/vy)/(E*/Yd)]−1/2 with α = 0.78 and Johnson's yield velocity vy can be solved for Yd. The result: 2089 ± 110 MPa at 25 °C, about 1901 MPa at 100 °C and 1825 ± 65 MPa at 200 °C, a fall of 10.39%. The glass softens: it trades impact resistance for energy dissipation.

6 · Simulation

To see the mechanism, I built a quarter-symmetric ABAQUS model (about 1.35 million C3D8I elements, 0.25 µm at the contact) with a coupled shear-transformation-zone / free-volume constitutive model. Its crater profile matched the confocal measurement at 300 m/s. At 400 m/s the deepest indentation came at 14.4 ns (25 °C) against 15.2 ns (200 °C), and separation at 23.6 against 25.6 ns: a longer contact, lower stress, greater plastic strain, and many more positive shear transformations at 200 °C.

7 · Limitations

Yield stress is inferred, not measured, so small velocity errors grow through v². Thermal gradients mean the true impact temperature is unknown, and adiabatic heating could not be measured. Above Tg the surface oxidised badly (we tried a vacuum, but the chamber needed was too large), so the range stopped at 200 °C.

8 · Conclusion

Temperature governs how a metallic glass meets an impact: from solid-like to fluid-like, with 10.4% lower dynamic yield strength and more energy absorbed. Unlike crystalline metals, it does not harden when hot.
